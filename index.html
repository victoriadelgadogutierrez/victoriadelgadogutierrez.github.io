Origin

My interest in engineering started with small builds. As a kid, I assembled simple robots from magnets, batteries, and basic circuits, using translucent LEGO pieces to color their lights. A seventh-grade visit to the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum gave that curiosity a direction. In the planetarium, I watched stars move across the dome. Outside, planes flew across the exhibit screens and hung just above my head. I left wanting to understand how we reach space and how I could help get us there.

First Mission

In 2022, I was selected for NASA’s Texas High School Aerospace Scholars program. Through its coursework, I studied how NASA technology moves into everyday products. As a manager in the Moonshot Experience, I researched radiation shielding for Gateway, part of the Artemis program. It was my first look at how spacecraft are designed to protect their crews, and it set my goal of working at Johnson Space Center.

How I Work

I do my best work on projects I can take from concept to hardware. I design components in SolidWorks and Fusion 360, and I use Python and MATLAB to verify them before fabrication. Once a part is built, I test it against the design intent. When the results differ, I trace the cause and revise the design. I want to bring this approach to spacecraft components, mechatronics, and propulsion systems.

Rover Robotic Arm and End Effector

When our rover came in over its weight and budget limits, I learned to treat weight as a design input from the first sketch.

LUSI is Lehigh’s student rover team, with more than 35 members across five subteams. The team competes in the University Rover Challenge at the Mars Desert Research Station in Utah, where the arm must lift 5 kg, open a latched drawer, insert a USB-C connector, and type on a keyboard.

As Wrist Engineer, I redesigned the wrist of our five-degree-of-freedom arm to cut weight and cost. I sized components against torque and weight limits and reduced the motor count by integrating linear actuators, while preserving the full range of motion. When shortened arm segments shifted the arm’s center of gravity, I worked with the other subteams on the fix and modeled the full arm assembly in SolidWorks to verify its range of motion.

The previous 3D-printed gripper used PETG gears that failed under load. I co-designed its replacement with the team: aluminum claws opened and closed by a linear actuator. As End Effector Team Lead, I now direct a team of four responsible for the gripper mechanics and for planning how the end effector completes each competition task, including typing. I delegate responsibilities, organize our design workflow, and train incoming members in SolidWorks and 3D printing through team workshops.

Low-Cost sEMG Electrode for Dysphagia Screening

A low-cost sensor only matters if it works on real people. Running a clinical study taught me that.

Dysphagia, or difficulty swallowing, affects an estimated 590 million people worldwide. Our team developed a surface EMG electrode made from wheat starch hydrogel. It costs about $0.03 per unit to make, compared with about $0.45 for a common commercial electrode. I lead mechanical design on the four-person team building a low-cost wearable sEMG device for dysphagia screening.

I formulated the hydrogel and validated it through impedance testing. I designed the seven-layer electrode stack in SolidWorks and laser cut the copper tape, foam, and adhesive layers that hold it to the skin. I also wrote the Python pipeline that detects each swallow in the signal and calculates five metrics per swallow.

In May 2026, I traveled with the team to Almaty, Kazakhstan, to run the electrode study with a clinical partner, the first study in Kazakhstan to record swallowing-related muscle activity with sEMG. I ran 30 impedance tests with clinical users to compare our electrode against commercial electrodes. Our electrode recorded stronger signals than the commercial electrodes, with more variability, which we believe is partly due to the adhesive. The manuscript has been submitted to BMES.

I am now designing the first version of a wearable device: a compact pod worn under the chin, with a custom PCB, a rigid case, and a silicone shell. I am also designing the mold that will cast the shell, for CNC machining, and the device’s PCB in Fusion 360.

Settling Dynamics of Disk-Shaped Particles

Microplastics in the ocean are mostly flat fragments and films, not spheres, so I modeled them as disks.

I wrote a Python simulation that tracks a disk-shaped particle through three flow fields: uniform flow, a deep-water wave, and a Taylor-Green vortex array. The particle’s velocity comes from the Maxey-Riley equation with drag and gravity terms, integrated with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) scheme. The disk’s tilt angle evolves under Jeffery’s equation. I switched individual force terms on and off to isolate how each one affects the trajectory.

In uniform flow, the tilt angle stays fixed. Disks falling broadside and edgewise reached their theoretical terminal velocities, which I checked against hand calculations to validate the model. Orientation alone changed settling speed by 50%, and a disk tilted at 45° drifted sideways, which a sphere cannot do.

In the Taylor-Green vortex, velocity gradients rotated the disk through about four full turns during its descent, so no single orientation controlled its settling. Across Stokes numbers, the lowest-inertia particles (St = 0.03) were swept into downwelling regions and settled 6.2 times faster than in still fluid. By St = 0.3, that speedup faded and drag and gravity dominated.

I also built a MATLAB tool to plot the velocity field of each flow. The next steps are adding the added-mass force and fluid-inertia torque, then extending the model to turbulent flow.

Electrochemical CO₂ Reduction to Formic Acid

A catalyst’s surface decides a lot about what a reaction produces. My first research project showed me how much.

Electrochemical CO₂ reduction uses electricity to convert CO₂ into useful chemicals. This project targeted formic acid, which has uses in hydrogen storage and fuel cells.

We used cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) on copper as an affordable, tunable base catalyst. We then modified it with silver and with tin and compared formic acid production across the three electrodes.

I carried out the work at every stage: electrode synthesis, electrochemical testing, Raman spectroscopy, and SEM imaging. Across more than 30 electrochemical reactor experiments, I varied the operating conditions and improved formic acid selectivity by 60%. I used SEM and Raman spectroscopy to verify carbon phase transformations.

Formic acid production increased at more negative potentials and peaked at −1.4 V. The tin-modified electrode produced the most formic acid. Raman spectra confirmed that the copper oxides reduced during the reaction. SEM images showed that the tin-modified surface kept its wire-like structure, while unmodified Cu₂O collapsed.

I also worked on a cobalt catalyst project. I synthesized cobalt on nickel foam (2Co@NF), electrodeposited cobalt hydroxide, and oxidized it electrochemically to oxyhydroxide. Smooth cobalt substrates held the deposit poorly compared with rough nickel and did not oxidize fully, so I recommended roughening cobalt with sandpaper before deposition.

After the fellowship, Dr. Baltrusaitis recommended me to another faculty lab and invited me back to work on a new project in electrochemical wastewater separation.

Mortise Lock Reverse Engineering

A model is not finished until it moves like the real thing. This project taught me to check my CAD against the physical part at every step.

I disassembled a commercial mortise lock and measured each component with calipers. For features the calipers could not reach, I traced reference photos inside the sketches.

I modeled all ten components from scratch with fully defined sketches, including a torsion spring built from a helix and sweep and M4 threads cut without the Thread feature. The assembly reproduces the lock’s motion: rotating the cam drives the tongue and the latch bolt.

I also analyzed fit, clearance, material selection, and manufacturing method to evaluate how each affects the lock’s performance.

I printed every part on a single build plate in under three hours, for $2.29 of PLA. I iterated on dimensions until the printed lock operated with the original key and springs, and its parts are interchangeable with the original metal components. Faculty selected my model as the sole reference model out of 63 projects, to guide future CAD students.
